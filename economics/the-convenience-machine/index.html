Operating manual

How the machine works

The Convenience Machine is an interactive model about one simple question: when a business makes life more convenient for a customer, where does the work and uncertainty go?

1. What is this?

Imagine that you want groceries. You could travel to a store, find the products, wait, carry everything home and spend your own time doing it.

A delivery service lets you avoid much of that work. But the work does not magically disappear. Someone still has to predict demand, hold inventory, prepare orders, move them and deliver them.

The central idea

Convenience often means that the customer experiences less friction because the system absorbs more friction.

2. What are you doing?

You are operating the convenience system. Move the sliders and watch the machine respond.

Change an inputAlter demand, speed, capacity, prediction or customer conditions.
Watch the live outputThe numbers on the right update immediately while you move a slider.
Change the strategyChoose whether friction is relocated, destroyed or pre-positioned against.
InvestigateUse stress tests, the flywheel, the counterfactual and Advanced mode to go deeper.

There is no single correct setting. The purpose is to discover the trade-offs.

3. What does every slider mean?

Demand densityHow concentrated potential orders are. Think of customers spread across a huge area versus many customers close together.
Order frequencyHow repeatedly customers generate orders. More repetition can improve how efficiently the system is used.
Willingness to payThe maximum modeled value the customer places on escaping friction. It is a ceiling on modeled customer value; it is not automatically the price paid.
UrgencyHow strongly timing matters to the customer. A more urgent customer places more modeled value on avoiding waiting.
Delivery promiseHow aggressively the business promises speed. Higher values represent a faster, more demanding promise. It is a model variable, not a literal number of minutes.
Inventory proximityHow close useful inventory is positioned to demand. Higher proximity can reduce physical friction and improve responsiveness.
Courier capacityHow much fulfilment capacity the system has available during the modeled period.
Prediction accuracyHow accurately the system anticipates demand before it arrives. Better prediction can reduce uncertainty and improve resource positioning.

4. What do the live numbers mean?

OrdersThe modeled number of orders generated during the operating period.
CapacityThe modeled amount of available fulfilment capacity.
UtilizationHow much available capacity is being used. For example, 70 orders using 100 capacity units means 70% utilization.
System costThe modeled operating cost assigned to one order under the current assumptions.
Customer valueThe modeled value the customer receives from escaping friction. It is not necessarily the price the customer pays.
RevenueThe modeled revenue generated per order from the current customer-value and willingness-to-pay assumptions.
ProfitRevenue minus system cost. A negative number means the modeled system costs more per order than the modeled revenue captures.
Failure riskA modeled operating-risk measure derived from utilization, prediction, speed and strategy. It is not a real-world probability forecast.

5. What does the operating state mean?

The machine turns the current operating conditions into four descriptive states:

HEALTHYThe modeled system is operating without the stronger pressure conditions used by the model.
TIGHTUtilization, risk or uncertainty has moved into a meaningful pressure range.
STRAINEDUtilization, risk or uncertainty is materially high.
OVERLOADEDDemand reaches or exceeds modeled capacity, or failure risk reaches the model's highest pressure threshold.

6. What is the Friction Absorption section?

The lower part of the Market block translates the model into the article's main idea: friction does not simply vanish; it is absorbed somewhere in the system.

TimeWaiting friction is addressed through the speed and fulfilment system.
Physical effortWork the customer would have performed is shifted toward inventory and fulfilment operations.
UncertaintyPrediction and positioning absorb some uncertainty before the customer experiences it.
System pressureCapacity and utilization determine how much operational pressure the system is carrying.

7. What are the three friction strategies?

RelocateThe customer's work still exists, but workers and infrastructure perform it instead. Example: you do not drive to the store; a courier does.
DestroyA step is eliminated rather than transferred. The process itself becomes simpler.
Pre-positionResources are spent before demand appears. Inventory or capacity is placed in anticipation of future demand.
The trade-offPre-positioning can improve responsiveness, but resources can be wasted when predictions are wrong.

8. What are the presets?

Presets are simply starting situations. They do not create special rules or a different model. They move several sliders at once so you can quickly explore a low-density city, a dense neighborhood, an urgent customer or a casual customer.

9. What are the stress tests?

A stress test temporarily changes one part of the current system and reports what happens. It does not permanently change your sliders.

Double the promisePush the speed promise upward.
Remove densityReduce the concentration of demand.
Remove capacityReduce available courier capacity.
Damage predictionReduce prediction accuracy.
Pull inventory backReduce inventory proximity.
Increase urgencyMake timing matter more.

10. What is the flywheel?

Sometimes convenience can reinforce itself. More convenience can encourage more usage. More usage can create greater demand density. Greater density can make prediction and fulfilment more efficient. That can lower unit cost and make more convenience possible.

Convenience → Behavior → Density → Prediction → Unit cost → Convenience

This is a possible reinforcing loop, not a guarantee that every convenience business experiences it.

11. What is the counterfactual?

It asks a simple question: what would the modeled customer value look like without the convenience layer? The comparison gives you a baseline for thinking about the value created by the system.

12. What happens when I switch to Advanced?

Nothing about the underlying machine changes. Advanced mode opens the same model and exposes more of its internal structure. When you click Advanced, the page automatically takes you to the beginning of the Advanced section so you know that the mode changed.

Unit economics decompositionShows the individual cost components and reconciles them to total system cost.
Customer value decompositionShows time value, physical effort value, uncertainty value, reliability penalty and the willingness-to-pay ceiling.
Operating stateShows utilization, slack, uncertainty, efficiency, customer surplus, margin, bottleneck and strategy.
Model equationsShows the mathematical relationships used to generate the outputs.
Marginal sensitivityChanges one input by a defined amount while keeping the other inputs fixed, then compares the resulting profit per order.
Scenario comparisonRuns named scenarios through the same model. It is descriptive, not a ranking of the scenarios.
Model integrity auditChecks whether the implementation satisfies its own mathematical identities and bounds.

13. The mathematics in plain English

The machine follows a chain:

Inputs → Operating conditions → Cost / value → Revenue → Profit / risk
Utilization = Orders ÷ Capacity × 100

If 70 orders use 100 capacity units, utilization is 70%.

Profit = Revenue − System cost
Customer surplus = Customer value − Revenue
Customer value = clamp(Time + Effort + Uncertainty − Reliability penalty, 0, WTP)

When the model says clamp, it simply means that the result cannot go below or above a specified boundary. For example, if calculated customer value were $18 but willingness to pay were $7, the modeled customer value cannot exceed $7.

14. What do the numbers NOT mean?

Precise arithmetic is not the same thing as real-world prediction.

The machine is deterministic: the same inputs always produce the same outputs, and Advanced mode exposes the equations. But the coefficients are illustrative modeling assumptions designed to isolate the mechanism discussed in the article.

A real delivery company would also have wages, fuel, rent, taxes, spoilage, cancellations, returns, customer acquisition, financing, competition, geography, regulation and many other variables. Those are deliberately outside this model.

So the calculations are precise; the assumptions are illustrative. The outputs should not be interpreted as forecasts for a real company.
